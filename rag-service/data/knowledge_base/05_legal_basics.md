# Legal Basics for Small Businesses

## Choosing a Business Structure

Your business structure affects your personal liability, taxes, and ability to raise capital. Choose carefully and consult an attorney.

### Sole Proprietorship

The simplest structure. You and the business are legally the same entity. No formation paperwork required beyond local business licenses. You report business income on your personal tax return.

Downside: you are personally liable for all business debts and lawsuits. Your personal assets including home and savings are at risk.

### Partnership

Two or more people share ownership. General partnerships are simple to form but all partners have unlimited personal liability. Limited partnerships have at least one general partner with unlimited liability and limited partners whose liability is restricted to their investment.

Always create a written partnership agreement covering profit sharing, decision-making authority, dispute resolution, and exit procedures. Verbal agreements lead to disputes.

### Limited Liability Company (LLC)

Combines the liability protection of a corporation with the tax simplicity of a partnership. Members' personal assets are generally protected from business debts and lawsuits.

An LLC requires articles of organization filed with your state, an operating agreement, and annual fees. It can have one or more members and elect different tax treatments.

### Corporation (C-Corp and S-Corp)

Corporations are separate legal entities that offer the strongest liability protection. They can issue stock and attract investors. C-Corps pay corporate income tax and shareholders pay tax on dividends. S-Corps avoid double taxation by passing income through to shareholders.

Corporations require more formality: board meetings, corporate minutes, bylaws, and annual reports. Best for businesses planning to raise significant investment or go public.

## Contracts and Agreements

### Essential Business Contracts

Terms of Service: governs the relationship between your business and customers. Covers payment terms, liability limitations, dispute resolution, and acceptable use.

Service agreements: clearly define scope of work, deliverables, timelines, payment schedule, and termination conditions. Always have a written agreement before starting work.

Non-disclosure agreements: protect confidential information when sharing with employees, contractors, or potential partners.

Employment contracts: define job responsibilities, compensation, benefits, confidentiality obligations, and termination procedures.

### Contract Best Practices

Put everything in writing. Verbal agreements are difficult to enforce. Use plain language — overly complex legal jargon can create ambiguity.

Include a dispute resolution clause specifying whether disputes will be handled through mediation, arbitration, or litigation, and in which jurisdiction.

Have an attorney review contracts before signing, especially for large transactions, long-term commitments, or unusual terms.

## Intellectual Property Protection

### Trademarks

Protect your business name, logo, and slogans. Register trademarks with the relevant national office. Federal registration provides nationwide protection and the right to use the registered symbol.

Conduct a trademark search before naming your business or products to avoid infringement claims.

### Copyrights

Protect original creative works: written content, software code, designs, photographs, and marketing materials. Copyright protection is automatic upon creation, but registration provides stronger legal standing.

### Trade Secrets

Protect proprietary processes, customer lists, formulas, and business strategies through confidentiality agreements and internal security measures. Unlike patents, trade secrets can last indefinitely if properly protected.

### Patents

Protect inventions and unique processes. Patent applications are complex and expensive. Consult a patent attorney if you have a genuinely novel invention with commercial potential.

## Business Licenses and Permits

Research which licenses and permits your business needs at federal, state, and local levels. Common requirements include a general business license, professional licenses for regulated industries, health department permits for food businesses, and zoning permits for home-based or retail businesses.

Renew licenses on time. Operating without required permits can result in fines or forced closure.

## Regulatory Compliance

### Data Privacy

If you collect customer data, you must comply with relevant privacy laws such as GDPR for European customers, CCPA for California residents, and other applicable regulations.

Implement a privacy policy on your website. Obtain consent before collecting data. Provide mechanisms for customers to access, correct, or delete their data.

### Employment Law

Comply with minimum wage, overtime, and workplace safety regulations. Classify workers correctly as employees or independent contractors. Misclassification can result in significant penalties.

Maintain required workplace postings and provide mandated benefits. Keep employment records for the required retention period.

### Industry-Specific Regulations

Research regulations specific to your industry. Food businesses need health inspections. Financial services require specific licenses. Healthcare businesses must comply with patient privacy laws. Construction businesses need bonding and insurance.

## When to Hire a Lawyer

Hire a business attorney when forming your business, drafting or reviewing significant contracts, facing a lawsuit or legal threat, buying or selling a business, dealing with intellectual property issues, or navigating complex regulatory requirements.

Build a relationship with a business attorney before you urgently need one. Many offer initial consultations for free or at reduced rates.
