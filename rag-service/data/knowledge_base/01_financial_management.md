# Financial Management for Small and Medium Businesses

## Cash Flow Management

Cash flow is the lifeblood of any small business. More businesses fail due to cash flow problems than lack of profitability. Effective cash flow management means ensuring that the money coming in exceeds the money going out on a consistent basis.

### Key Cash Flow Strategies

Monitor your cash flow weekly, not monthly. Create a 13-week rolling cash flow forecast to anticipate shortfalls before they happen. This gives you enough time to arrange financing or adjust spending.

Invoice immediately after delivering goods or services. Offer early payment discounts such as 2/10 net 30 to encourage faster payments. Follow up on overdue invoices within 48 hours of the due date.

Negotiate longer payment terms with suppliers. If you can get net 60 instead of net 30, you keep cash longer. Build strong supplier relationships to maintain flexibility during tight periods.

### Working Capital Optimization

Working capital equals current assets minus current liabilities. A healthy working capital ratio is between 1.2 and 2.0. Below 1.0 means you may struggle to pay short-term obligations.

Reduce inventory holding costs by implementing just-in-time ordering where possible. Excess inventory ties up cash that could be used for growth or emergencies.

## Budgeting for Small Businesses

### Creating an Effective Budget

Start with your fixed costs: rent, salaries, insurance, loan payments, and subscriptions. These are predictable and form your baseline.

Add variable costs: raw materials, marketing spend, utilities, and commissions. Use the previous 12 months as a baseline and adjust for planned changes.

Allocate revenue into categories: 50% to operating costs, 30% to growth investments, and 20% to reserves and debt repayment. Adjust these ratios based on your business stage.

### Zero-Based Budgeting

Instead of adjusting last year's budget, start from zero each quarter. Every expense must be justified anew. This prevents budget bloat and forces you to evaluate whether each spending category still delivers value.

## Financial Statements Every Owner Should Understand

### Profit and Loss Statement (Income Statement)

This shows your revenue minus expenses over a period. Review it monthly to track trends. Pay attention to your gross profit margin, which is revenue minus cost of goods sold divided by revenue. Most healthy small businesses maintain gross margins above 50%.

### Balance Sheet

The balance sheet shows what you own (assets), what you owe (liabilities), and your equity at a specific point in time. Review it quarterly. Watch your debt-to-equity ratio — above 2.0 suggests heavy reliance on borrowed money.

### Cash Flow Statement

Different from your P&L because it tracks actual cash movement. A profitable business can still run out of cash if customers pay slowly or inventory builds up.

## Bookkeeping Best Practices

Separate personal and business finances completely. Use a dedicated business bank account and credit card. This simplifies tax preparation and provides clearer financial insights.

Reconcile your bank statements monthly. Categorize every transaction consistently. Use accounting software like QuickBooks, Xero, or Wave to automate categorization and reporting.

Keep receipts digitally. Use apps that photograph and categorize receipts automatically. The IRS and tax authorities require documentation for all business deductions.

## Key Financial Ratios to Track Monthly

Current ratio: current assets divided by current liabilities. Target 1.5 or higher.
Gross profit margin: gross profit divided by revenue. Track trends monthly.
Net profit margin: net income divided by revenue. Healthy SMBs target 10-20%.
Accounts receivable turnover: how quickly customers pay. Calculate by dividing revenue by average accounts receivable.
Burn rate: monthly cash expenditure minus monthly revenue. Critical for startups and growth-phase businesses.

## Emergency Fund for Businesses

Every small business should maintain 3-6 months of operating expenses in a separate savings account. This buffer protects against unexpected downturns, late-paying customers, or emergency repairs. Build it gradually by setting aside 5-10% of monthly revenue until you reach your target.
